<p>Nonetheless, in the past, Columbia allowed neighbors to cross the campus as a scenic shortcut through the neighborhood. Nearby nursery schools used the campus green as a place for children to explore and play.</p>

<p>As a <a href="https://www.amherst.edu/people/facstaff/adsarat">college professor of law</a> and political science, and an <a href="https://www.joneslibrary.org/trustees">elected official in the western Massachusetts town where I teach</a>, the Columbia campus controversy feels familiar. These <a href="https://capturingcambridge.org/explore-by-theme/town-and-gown-the-struggle-and-story-in-cambridge/">“town-and-gown” disputes</a> have, from time to time, raised questions about what universities owe the community beyond their gates. </p>

<h2>Town-and-gown conflicts</h2>

<p>From at least the 1950s until 2024, Columbia had an open campus policy. In 1953, New York City <a href="https://www.college.columbia.edu/cct/issue/fall17/article/uniting-columbias-campus">approved closing the campus’s central West 116th Street</a> stretch to cars, with the understanding that pedestrians would be able to walk across the campus. </p>

<p>The recent closure stirred up tensions between the school and its neighbors. But such strains are as old as universities themselves. Some <a href="https://historyofeducation.net/2020/09/02/the-bad-tempered-backstory-of-town-versus-gown/">commentators suggest</a> that conflict between universities and their surrounding communities can be traced back to medieval Europe. </p>

<p>“The early universities in cities such as Bologna and Paris took a very different form to the institutions we know today. They were loose communities of masters and scholars who lived amongst the people of the town,” the education historian <a href="https://historyofeducation.net/2020/09/02/the-bad-tempered-backstory-of-town-versus-gown/">Harley Richardson wrote in 2020</a>. </p>

<p>Townspeople often resented those scholars and students because “They couldn’t understand the scholarly Latin spoken by the students” or whether “the long gowns they wore” were “intended to convey a superior social status,” Richardson wrote.</p>

<p>One of the most notorious town-gown incidents that turned violent <a href="https://www.oxfordhistory.org.uk/mayors/government/scholastica.html">occurred</a> in Oxford, England, in February 1354 on so-called St. Scholastica Day, <a href="https://www.vaticannews.va/en/saints/02/10/st--scolastica--virgin--sister-di-s--benedetto.html">honoring an Italian saint who founded the order of the Benedictine nuns</a>.</p>

<p>Trouble began in a local tavern when some students complained loudly and aggressively about the poor quality of the wine they were served. Things escalated when the tavern-keeper, who was also the mayor of Oxford, responded with insults. A fight broke out and drew in a large number of townspeople and students who rushed to the scene. It took the intervention of the king to restore order. </p>

<p>Conflicts between universities and their local towns or cities <a href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1933/09/16/that-was-college-life">have also been very much a part</a> of the story of higher education in the United States. </p>

<p>Most often <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/marvinkrislov/2026/11/20/how-universities-help-shape-the-cities-they-call-home/">these disputes have focused</a> on issues like where a university should build a dormitory or whether it should pay fees to the city where it is located. </p>

<p>And as in England, these conflicts have occasionally turned violent. </p>

<p>One such incident happened in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1806, when riots broke out <a href="https://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/issues/01_03/town_gown.html">between Yale University students</a> and locals who were on their way to church.</p>

<h2>Universities’ civic responsibilities</h2>

<p>What, if anything, <a href="https://podcasts.apple.com/in/podcast/what-do-universities-owe-society-with-ron-daniels/id1642334178?i=1000733876703&amp;l=pa">do universities owe their communities</a>? </p>

<p>The popular phrase “<a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s42438-022-00341-0">the ivory tower</a>” captures one vision of a university. It suggests that universities serve society by being left alone to conduct research and educate students, disengaged from the larger community’s immediate concerns. In this view, people outside of universities benefit indirectly from the inventions and innovations that universities produce, as well as from the jobs they create. </p>

<p>Another vision, which came to prominence in the 1960s, <a href="https://digitalcommons.unomaha.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1127&amp;context=slcehighered">called on universities to be good citizens</a> of their surrounding communities. This view <a href="https://www.facingsouth.org/2025/08/what-do-universities-owe-communities-interview-davarian-baldwin">urged university leaders</a> to invest in their neighborhoods. </p>

<p>Colby College, in Waterville, Maine, <a href="https://news.colby.edu/story/a-new-report-measures-colbys-economic-impact/">exemplifies this approach</a>. It has spent US$265 million on construction and other investment projects over the past <a href="https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2023/01/16/colby-revitalizes-downtown-waterville">several years</a>, with an eye on revitalizing <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2020/02/25/business/colleges-downtowns-investment.html">its struggling downtown district</a>. </p>

<p>Others schools, including the <a href="https://stories.nd.edu/stories/rolling-out-the-welcome-mat/?utm_source=chatgpt.com">University of Notre Dame</a>, the <a href="https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/penn-medicine-invests-3-million-in-south-and-southwest-philadelphia-to-support-storm-recovery-initiative-led-by-deeply-rooted">University of Pennsylvania</a>, the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2017/08/15/realestate/commercial/usc-village-los-angeles-campus.html">University of Southern California</a> and the <a href="https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2021/11/421841/ucsf-launches-community-investment-program-support-housing-and-minority-owned">University of California, San Francisco</a>, have invested substantial amounts of money in <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/higher-education-cities-hidden-asset/">developing their towns and cities</a> and supporting neighbors. Trinity College in Hartford, Connecticut, for example, announced in September 2026 that <a href="https://www.trincoll.edu/news/trinity-college-unveils-major-financial-aid-initiatives/">it would pay for the full tuition</a>, housing and meals for local residents who are admitted as students. </p>

<p>Some universities <a href="https://www.fivecolleges.edu/academics/community-based-learning">have been sending students out into the communities around them</a> as one way to support their city. For example, Columbia Journalism School students <a href="https://journalism.columbia.edu/CJS2030/LocalNonprofitNews">are often tasked</a> with developing a reporting project in a particular New York City neighborhood.</p>

<p>And some leaders in higher education now <a href="https://www.wesleyan.edu/about/news/2026/07/blog/higher-ed-at-americas-250.html">talk openly</a>  about <a href="https://www.amherst.edu/news/news_releases/2023/november/governor-healey-and-president-elliott-talk-democracy">orienting their schools</a> toward the promotion of the greater good.</p>

<h2>Closing gates or building bridges</h2>

<p>A university investing in its surrounding community does not guarantee the end of tensions, which to me seem an inevitable part of the higher education landscape.</p>

<p>But when universities show their communities that civic responsibility is core to their mission, they build goodwill and offer a way of bridging divides when conflicts occur. </p>

<p>I don’t know how the Columbia campus dispute will resolve. But as newly elected New York State Assemblyman <a href="https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/news/2026/11/19/students-neighbors-question-columbia-over-locked-campus-entrances">Eli Northrop observed</a>: “Columbia is successful when Morningside Heights is successful and when the relationship between the neighborhood and the university is good.”</p><img src="https://counter.theconversation.com/content/297102/count.gif" alt="The Conversation" width="1" height="1" />
